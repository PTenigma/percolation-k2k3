BBC World Service · The World Today · 10 May 2006

How methamphetamine affects the brain

Max Pearson · Presenter

View story image ↗
Listen while you readClick any timestamp to start at that point
← Original archive entry and research figure
PEARSON

On the streets it's called speed, crank, ice, zip, crystal, and meth. Its real name is methamphetamine, and it's one of the most addictive drugs on the planet. The American Drug Enforcement Agency says it's now used more than heroin and cocaine combined. One of the highest levels of methamphetamine abuse is in Los Angeles. Dr Paul Thompson is a neuroscientist researching the effects of the drug at the University of California in Los Angeles. He's on the line now. Just how damaging is this to the human brain?

THOMPSON

Well, good morning. Well, we've actually been looking at this for some time using clinical brain MRI scans. One of the things that we saw is that about 10% of the brain tissue is actually damaged and gone after people have been regularly using this drug. Now that's equivalent to the amount of tissue loss in Alzheimer's disease. It produces profound depression, memory loss, symptoms of anxiety. And even though in the short term it's giving people essentially a lot of energy and a rush and euphoria, over the long term it actually produces devastating symptoms.

PEARSON

When you say over the long term and regular use, how long are you talking about? Because 10% of brain tissue loss is an astonishing figure.

THOMPSON

It is quite remarkable. We think that one percent of the brain's tissue is lost per year. So these are people who are regularly smoking methamphetamine, maybe every few days. A frightening statistic is that five percent of teenagers in the United States have used methamphetamine at some point, and just through regular use, 1% per year of your brain tissue is lost. This coincides with memory decline, anxiety, and over a very, very long term there are [?] tremors, a little bit like Parkinson's disease, where there's obviously a complete inability to control motor function.

PEARSON

And a characteristic even in the short term is, well, a personality change.

THOMPSON

Absolutely. So this drug provokes people to do things that they wouldn't normally do. We know that many of the people that are arrested in Los Angeles, about 40% of them, have been using methamphetamine. Now this means that the energy, the drive, the inability to think through risks causes a lot of violent crimes. It's involved in a lot of violent incidents and murders in the United States. And we're worried that as this drug is spreading throughout the US and overseas, this could produce a massive increase in violent crime.

PEARSON

Right. As a social problem, how do you counter a drug which gives a euphoric high, which can cause personalities to change, lead to violence, and yet is so readily available, so relatively cheap, and, as the DEA says now, is the biggest and most widely used drug on the planet?

THOMPSON

Absolutely. Well, there are two ways. One is to try and shut down the labs that are producing it. The drug is completely man-made. It's usually made from chemicals in cold medicine, which are in extremely wide availability. In the United States there's actually been a restriction on the availability of cold medicine to make it more difficult to synthesize the drug. The second thing is to combat its effects. So we know that 35 million people worldwide use methamphetamine regularly. Now that's a devastating loss of mental function and danger worldwide. And just through therapy, and trying to understand people's addiction, and trying to essentially realize that this is causing progressive brain damage: that's the only way that we think that we can get through to people that this is causing progressive brain tissue loss.

PEARSON

In a word, do you believe anything like enough is being done to win the campaign against methamphetamine?

THOMPSON

Well, I think part of the problem is it's not understood how devastating it is. So most people think of stimulants like caffeine and coffee and think that the effect is only psychological. The problem with methamphetamine is it's like something that's 10,000 times as powerful as coffee that actually kills the brain cells that it's stimulating, and they can never be replaced. So I think part of the message is just realize how progressively devastating this drug is, and the brain tissue that's damaged by it can never be replaced, and that's basically the tragedy that we're looking at today.

PEARSON

Dr Paul Thompson from the University of California in Los Angeles, thanks for joining us.

BBC radio · 6 November 2001

How genes shape the human brain

James Naughtie · Presenter (provisional attribution)

NAUGHTIE

I suppose the starting point is that most people would say we've always sort of assumed that there's some inherited element. In other words, you'd expect Einstein's children to get a few O-levels. But what have you proved that we didn't know?

THOMPSON

Absolutely, I think that's a terrific point. Basically, we're very interested in understanding the basis of inherited disease and how similar brain structure is in families. And the nature of the question is often a question of degree. I mean, we know that there are some inherited factors and some features of the brain that are determined by the environment or learning. And one of the surprises in this study is that there are certain elements of the brain that are extremely determined by our genetics. And examples are the language cortex, the part of the brain that controls language. This is very, very similar in twins; in brothers and sisters, slightly less similar. And one of the things that we think about that is that it could explain why some diseases like dyslexia, autism, which has very, very severe familial inheritance and also severe speech problems, could be a disease of the language cortex, and that's also an area that's very hard to — [crosstalk]

NAUGHTIE

That's interesting, so it leads you into all sorts of other areas—

THOMPSON

Absolutely.

NAUGHTIE

—other things. I mean, other bits of the brain, without wanting to reduce it to simplistic a level, other brain functions which seem to be much less affected by genetic elements?

THOMPSON

Absolutely, and this is very interesting. There are parts of the brain that are exceptionally susceptible to learning and other sorts of features. We know the brain is tremendously dynamic, and we think of the brain as a jigsaw. There are some areas in the frontal cortex, that's just behind the eyes, where we know that genes really pretty much control the structure of what's going on there. Other areas: the hippocampus, that's an area that we know is involved in learning and memory, and every time you make a decision to learn or even listen to this radio show, obviously there are tremendous changes going on there. And what's happening is our genes are making new proteins and basically building the physical structure of our brains as we're learning particular things.

NAUGHTIE

So in the simplest possible terms, what is it that we get from our inherited characteristics most noticeably, and what do we develop ourselves?

THOMPSON

Well, that's a terrific question. One of the things that seems clear is that language and speech and our communication abilities are very strongly inherited. Also a component of our problem-solving abilities is also inherited. But it's only about 10 to 15 percent of the variation in that trait. Which means to say that the effects of nurture, the effects of learning and other non-genetic factors, have a tremendous importance in building the brain structure and building intelligence and performance on tests.

HAMILTON

Thompson knows that because he was part of a team that studied the brains and intelligence of 92 people. The team conducted the usual IQ tests on each person, then they did the scans that show information pathways. These scans measure the structural integrity of white matter. The greater the structural integrity, the faster nerve impulses travel. Thompson says the link between brain speed and intelligence was pretty clear.

THOMPSON

When you say someone's quick-thinking, it's genuinely true that the impulses are going faster, and they're just much more efficient at processing information and then making a decision based on it.

HAMILTON

The study also found that genetic factors play a big role in how fast your brain works. Thompson's team figured this out because the 92 people in their study were all twins. This meant the researchers could tease out genes associated with the structural integrity of white matter. And it turned out many of these genes were also associated with intelligence. HAIER

We know that intelligence has some genetic component. And what the Thompson study is showing is that a large part of the genetic aspect of intelligence has to do with the white matter tracts that connect different parts of the brain.

The wires between the brain cells, the connections, the things that you can modify throughout life: they change and they improve through your 40s and 50s and 60s. And it's a much more positive note than the sort of old orthodox view of the brain, which was that you have all the brain cells you'll ever have by the age of three, they can never be replaced, and you have to use it or lose it, so to speak.

Singapore radio · Frontiers · Probably February 2003

Mapping Alzheimer’s disease

Bharati Jagdish · Presenter

View story image ↗
Listen while you readClick any timestamp to start at that point
← Original archive entry and research figure

♪ Introductory music

JAGDISH

This week on Frontiers: brain mapping technology that is set to help with early detection and treatment of Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative diseases. Welcome to the programme, I'm Bharati Jagdish.

JAGDISH

Alzheimer's disease afflicts about 10% of people over the age of 65. It occurs when brain lesions called amyloid plaques and tangles accumulate in the brains of Alzheimer's patients, and are associated with memory loss, disorientation and an inability to cope with everyday life as brain cells progressively die. Now scientists at the University of California in the US and the University of Queensland in Australia are using a powerful new imaging analysis technique to track the progress of Alzheimer's disease. Brain mapping technology, which detects very fine changes in MRI brain scans, has allowed researchers to create three-dimensional video maps showing how the disease systematically engulfs the brains of living patients. The tool, run on hardware from Silicon Graphics, could speed up diagnosis and the development of new therapies for the disease. Dr Paul Thompson is an assistant professor of neurology at the University of California's School of Medicine.

THOMPSON

This is an exciting breakthrough in dementia research. It's the first technique to actually visualize Alzheimer's disease as it spreads in the living brain. Now we can actually watch its progression, [and] you can begin to see if drugs are affecting the progress of the disease. Actually, we might begin to be able to detect it earlier, because you can actually see the physical signs of the disease earlier than the cognitive symptoms of memory decline that are usually symptomatic of dementia.

JAGDISH

Now, what is the conventional method of diagnosing Alzheimer's, and what were the problems associated with it?

THOMPSON

That's a great question. Usually the way that Alzheimer's is found in the clinic is by looking at performance on memory tests. It is quite difficult to distinguish between dementia and a healthy elderly pattern of aging. We all decline a little in abilities as we age, and also the much more rapid decline that has… [cut]

JAGDISH

This visualization technology: how precise is it exactly?

THOMPSON

One of the things we wanted to do is watch the physical spread of the progression of Alzheimer's disease. Now brain scanning, in particular MRI scanning, gives you very, very detailed pictures of the human brain. One of the problems is you only get one time point. It's a little bit like a Polaroid picture of somebody: you can't tell what's actually happening to them, or the changes that are happening to them. So what this technology does is it uses very, very fast computers provided by SGI Corporation. It actually reconstructs a movie, or a time-lapse movie, of the physical changes that happen over time, based on a sequence of brain scans.

JAGDISH

Now, you've published some new findings on Alzheimer's that came to light through the use of this analysis technique. Can you tell us more about your findings?

THOMPSON

One of the surprises with Alzheimer's is, although it's a brain disease, it doesn't affect all the brain in the same way. We know that the brain is organized into different parts that do different things. The memory area of the brain — this is the area just below the ears — this is losing a tremendous amount of tissue early in the disease. So this is an area to focus on for early detection. One of the next things that happens is there's almost a lava flow or a forest fire of cell loss that spreads through the brain as the disease progresses. And actually one of the puzzles that we found in this research, that is beginning to be found by other groups, is Alzheimer's actually doesn't affect certain areas of the brain. So some areas of the brain are involved with vision, with eyesight, with the sensations of touch or hearing. These are areas that Alzheimer's doesn't affect. So these first pictures of Alzheimer's hitting the brain are actually giving us an inkling as to what types of cells are preserved, and really trying to identify what it is about those cells that makes them immune or protected from the ravages of the disease.

JAGDISH

How will all of this information help in terms of diagnosis of Alzheimer's, and drug development and treatments in the future?

THOMPSON

These are two areas that are very, very useful for this technology. One application is detecting the disease early, and obviously a physical picture of the disease is going to give you a better impression of when a patient has early signs of dementia. For drug development, if you can actually see if a drug is physically slowing down the progression of the disease, it might be much easier to determine what its effects are locally in the brain, which drugs are most effective, and which drugs are most effective for particular patients.

JAGDISH

And that was Dr Paul Thompson, an assistant professor of neurology at the University of California's School of Medicine. The brain mapping technology can also help in the detection of other neurodegenerative diseases, such as schizophrenia and other childhood disorders that affect the growth of the brain. You've been listening to Frontiers, and I'm Bharati Jagdish. Do join me again, same time next week.

KCBS San Francisco · 16 April 2012

ENIGMA: Mapping brains worldwide

Susan Leigh Taylor · KCBS interviewer (attribution tentative)

View story image ↗
Listen while you readClick any timestamp to start at that point
← Original archive entry and research figure
KCBS ANCHOR

[clip begins mid-sentence] …and FM 106.9, KCBS. KCBS News Time 9:20. A movement toward the crowdsourcing of brain research. In what's being called the largest collaborative study of the brain to date, a number of scientists using imaging technology have for the first time focused on genes that they agree play a role in intelligence and memory. The research, in the latest issue of the journal Nature Genetics, involved more than 100 centers worldwide as well as 200 scientists. The lead researcher is Paul Thompson, a professor of neurology at UCLA, who joins us live this morning on the KCBS Newsline. Professor, thanks for your time this morning.

THOMPSON

Thank you, I appreciate you having me on the program.

KCBS ANCHOR

Is it overstating it to say that this is sort of like social networking meets science?

THOMPSON

Oh, I think that's a great analogy. I mean, we're all familiar with things like Wikipedia, which no one person could ever write in their lifetime. And this is sort of bringing social networking to neuroscience, where researchers around the world can all do their part to advance the cause of brain research.

KCBS ANCHOR

So how does this work?

THOMPSON

Well, it's kind of interesting. We were curious to find factors that help or harm the brain. Now we're all familiar with things like drugs and alcohol that harm the brain, and things like exercise and good diet that help the brain. But there are a lot of disorders of the brain that are partly genetic, things like Alzheimer's and to some extent depression. And we've really had a hard time finding drug targets, particularly genetic drug targets, that are increasing our risk for these diseases. And one of the reasons is our genetic code has a lot of variations in it, millions of them in fact. And we really have to dredge through masses and masses of data to try and find particular variants in the genetic code that, first of all, increase our risk for brain disease, and secondly we can target with drug treatment. So what we did is we asked all our colleagues — this was a movement started by myself and a group led by Nick Martin and Margie Wright in Brisbane, and a group led by Barbara Franke in the Netherlands. It was born out of frustration. We knew that we didn't have enough brain scans on our own to find systematic factors that affected the brain. So the project ENIGMA was born. ENIGMA is kind of an analogy with the code-breaking of the Nazi code in the Second World War. And the idea was, by screening brain scans worldwide, if anyone could contribute brain scans and DNA, we'd eventually be able to find single-letter changes in the genome that would be linked with loss of brain tissue, increased risk for disease. And one thing we stumbled upon is even a gene that affects our IQ by a little bit.

KCBS ANCHOR

Can this kind of shared information be expanded for other medical research, or is it uniquely suited for this type of research that you're doing?

THOMPSON

Well, you know, it's very good for this type of research because it's very digital. So genomic data can be shared with some privacy constraints — there are also legal constraints — and brain scans are also digital. So if I scan a person here at UCLA today, that scan can be sent to a collaborator in Australia, according to legal and privacy rules, of course. So it's a very efficient way of having a very large number of people working on a very large problem.

KCBS ANCHOR

I'm looking at one report that says about 21,000 people's scans were involved. That sounds like a lot, but is that a big enough sample group to get the kind of results you're looking for?

THOMPSON

It is. Now, that's a great point. So we're used to these studies in the news that say "obesity gene discovered" or "Alzheimer gene discovered", and typically they have about 20,000 people. And the reason that that's needed is that there are so many other factors other than our genome that affect our brain: just your educational level, your age; there are some gender differences in brain structure. And they are so complicated, and all of our life experiences are so complicated, that you really need to amass data to find the effects of these single-letter changes in the genome. And I think the huge surprise to us is that you wouldn't think on a brain scan you could see the effects of something as subtle as a single-letter change in the genome. And this is a thing that we all have. We all have variations in the genome. They don't kill us. They sometimes improve our resilience to disease, so some of these are good genetic changes and others of them are bad for us, but they're very, very tough to find without a massive database of data.

KCBS ANCHOR

Professor Thompson, thanks for your time, and congratulations on your research. Paul Thompson, a professor of neurology at UCLA, joining us this morning as it is the largest collaborative study of the brain to date. Stan, they took a number of scientists and got their imaging technology together for the first time and focused on genes that play a role in intelligence and memory. [clip ends]
